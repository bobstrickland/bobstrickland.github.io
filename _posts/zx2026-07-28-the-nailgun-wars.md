The first Big Carpentry firms to announce their "Nailgun First" hiring policy did so with the breathless confidence of prophets. R.D. Morton, the largest residential contractor on the eastern seaboard, sent a memo to every hiring manager: no applicant would be considered without attesting to five-plus years of nailgun experience. Never mind that pneumatic nailguns had only been widely available for six years.  Entire subdivisions had been built by carpenters with hammers, carpenters whose callused, steady hands could set a sixteen-penny nail in two blows without splitting the stud. The requirement went out anyway, a strange bureaucratic requirement that seemed to be less about the tool and more a way of saying that the old way of building was over.

The carpenters split, as carpenters do, into camps that talked past each other at the lumberyard and in the union halls. The anti-nailgun faction told stories about nails driven crooked at high speed; a framer in Ohio who put a sixteen-gauge nail through his palm because he rushed a corner he should have hand-set. They talked of the satisfaction of a hand-hammered joint and the muscle memory built over decades. The fear, rarely spoken outright, was that a tool which let a novice work fast would make their hard-won expertise worthless. The pro-nailgun faction rolled their eyes at this romanticism, pointing out that nailguns didn't build houses; carpenters did.  Refusing a tool because it was new is how you ended up the last guy on the job site still hand-sawing lumber while everyone else had moved to circular saws a generation earlier. Both sides had a point. Both sides also seemed to have stopped listening to the other sometime around the second beer.

<img src="/assets/images/nailguns.png" alt="Silly Billboard spoof" align="right" style="width: 300px; margin-left: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px;"> Into this stepped Artisan Nailguns, a manufacturer riding a wave of venture capital and true believers, with a billboard in Times Square forty feet tall: STOP HIRING CARPENTERS. THE ERA OF NAILGUNS IS HERE. It was, depending on who you asked, either a bold statement of an inevitable future or an almost comic act of self-sabotage from a company that sold tools to carpenters publicly wishing carpenters out of existence. As if a nailgun had ever once walked itself up a ladder or read a set of blueprints. The carpenters who saw it on their way to job sites weren't sure whether to laugh or start looking for a new trade.

Which is, in the end, why the shouting on both sides felt so strange to anyone standing a little outside it. The people who hated nailguns on principle were fighting a tool that was really just faster hammering. The people promoting nailguns as a revolution were fighting for a tool that by itself could neither measure a doorframe nor read a client's needs. The tool was never the argument. The argument was about who got to keep their job, who got to feel like their skill still mattered.

A good carpenter, the old ones liked to say, doesn't fall in love with any one tool. He keeps a full box: hammer, chisel, level, square, and yes, a nailgun for the long runs of studwork where speed is a virtue and nothing more. The mark of the trade was never which tool you swore loyalty to. It was knowing, wall by wall and joint by joint, which one the work actually called for - and having the standing, the wage, and the respect to make that call yourself, instead of having it made for you by someone forty feet up on a billboard.
