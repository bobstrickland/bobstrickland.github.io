# The Nailgun Wars

The first Big Carpentry firms to announce their "Nailgun First" hiring policy did so with the breathless confidence of prophets. Sawyer & Hatch, the largest residential contractor on the eastern seaboard, sent a memo to every hiring manager: no applicant would be considered without attesting to five-plus years of nailgun experience. Never mind that pneumatic nailguns had only been widely available for six years. Never mind that half the senior framers in the industry had built entire subdivisions with hammers and had callused, steady hands that could set a sixteen-penny nail in two blows without splitting the stud. The attestation requirement went out anyway, a strange bureaucratic fiction that everyone understood was less about the tool and more about a mood - a mood that said the old way of building was over, and if you couldn't prove fluency in the new way, you were already obsolete.

The carpenters split, as carpenters do, into camps that talked past each other at the lumberyard and in the union halls. The anti-nailgun faction told stories, some true, about nails driven crooked at high speed, about a framer in Ohio who put a sixteen-gauge finish nail through his palm because he'd rushed a corner he should have hand-set. They talked about the satisfaction of a hand-hammered joint, the muscle memory built over decades, the fear, not always spoken aloud, that a tool which let a novice work fast would make their hard-won expertise worthless. The pro-nailgun faction rolled their eyes at this as romanticism, pointed out that nailguns didn't build houses, carpenters did, and that refusing a tool because it was new was how you ended up the last guy on the job site still hand-sawing lumber while everyone else had moved to circular saws a generation earlier. Both sides had a point. Both sides also seemed to have stopped listening to the other sometime around the second beer.

What got lost, in all the shouting, was the plain truth every good carpenter already knew: a nailgun is extraordinary for framing a wall and useless for hanging a door that needs to swing true, terrible for fine trim work where a countersunk hand nail and a dab of putty will always look better, and actively dangerous if you reach for it out of habit instead of judgment. Nobody sane builds an entire house with one tool. The people arguing loudest (on both sides) often sounded like they'd never held a hammer or a nailgun, just an opinion.

Into this argument stepped Artisan Nailguns, a manufacturer riding a wave of venture capital and true believers, with a billboard in Times Square forty feet tall: STOP HIRING CARPENTERS. THE ERA OF NAILGUNS IS HERE. It was, depending on who you asked, either a bold statement of an inevitable future or an almost comic act of self-sabotage from a company that sold tools to carpenters publicly wishing carpenters out of existence. As if a nailgun had ever once, in the history of nailguns, walked itself up a ladder and read a set of blueprints. The carpenters who saw it on their way to job sites weren't sure whether to laugh or start looking for a new trade. Mostly they just felt a little sick, the particular nausea of watching someone who profits from your labor announce, cheerfully, that your labor is the problem.

The timing made it worse. For most of the preceding decade, the building industry had operated on the conviction that America faced a catastrophic, permanent housing shortage, and firms had hired accordingly: framers, finish carpenters, electricians, site managers, whole departments of estimators and schedulers, all brought on under the assumption that the boom was structural and would run forever. When it turned out the shortage had been overstated, inflated by cheap capital and a few bad models that everyone had cited without checking, the firms needed to shed the excess. They could have said: we overhired, we misjudged demand, we're sorry, here's severance. Instead they reached for the tool sitting right there on the table and said: we don't need as many of you anymore, because we have nailguns now. It was a tidier story. It required no admission of a strategic error made in the boardroom five years earlier. It let a bloated org chart look, retroactively, like an act of efficiency rather than an act of correction - a layoff dressed up as a leap into the future.

And that's the part that made the whole spectacle feel less like a debate about tools and more like a debate about something else entirely, wearing a tool's name as a mask. The nailgun didn't lay off the electricians. The nailgun didn't cut the office staff who'd never touched a framing nailer in their lives. A nailgun is a device for driving a fastener into wood faster than a swung hammer can. It can neither think nor hold opinions of its own about any of that, but it's easier to blame a machine for a decision than to blame the people who made it, and much easier to sell a machine as a revolution than to admit it's a solid, useful, limited tool being asked to carry the weight of an entire industry's excuses.

Which is, in the end, why the shouting on both sides felt so strange to anyone standing a little outside it. The people who hated nailguns on principle were fighting a tool that was, in most hands, just faster hammering. The people who worshipped nailguns as a revolution were fighting for a tool that could not by itself measure a doorframe, could not read a client's needs, could not decide that this particular joint called for glue and a hand-set brad instead of a sixteen-gauge finish nail fired at ninety psi. The tool was never the argument. The argument was about who got to keep their job, who got to feel like their skill still mattered, and who got to walk away with the profits while pointing at the nailgun and saying, look, it wasn't us.

A good carpenter, the old ones liked to say, doesn't fall in love with any one tool. He keeps a full box: hammer, chisel, level, square, and yes, a nailgun for the long runs of studwork where speed is a virtue and nothing more. The mark of the trade was never which tool you swore loyalty to. It was knowing, wall by wall and joint by joint, which one the work actually called for - and having the standing, the wage, and the respect to make that call yourself, instead of having it made for you by someone forty feet up on a billboard.
